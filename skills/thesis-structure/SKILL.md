# PhD Thesis Structure & Defense

## When to use
- Structuring new PhD dissertation
- Converting published papers into thesis-by-publication
- Preparing defense/viva/comprehensive exam
- Auditing dissertation against degree requirements

## Two formats — pick early

### A. Monograph (traditional)
One continuous document. Common in: humanities, pure math, Cambridge tradition.

### B. Thesis-by-publication (compilation)
Introduction + N published papers + synthesis. Common in: Nordic, Netherlands, Australia, increasingly MIT/Harvard STEM.

Check your program's rules BEFORE writing — policies vary by school, department, and year.

## Canonical monograph scaffold (STEM, 150-300 pp)

```
Front matter
├── Title page
├── Declaration of originality (Cambridge requires word count too)
├── Abstract (1 page, single-paragraph summary)
├── Acknowledgments
├── Contents + list of figures/tables/abbreviations

Body
├── Ch 1. Introduction (5-15%)
│   ├── Problem + why it matters
│   ├── Research questions/hypotheses
│   ├── Contributions (explicit, numbered)
│   └── Thesis roadmap
├── Ch 2. Background & Literature Review (10-20%)
│   ├── Scoped review — NOT everything
│   ├── Gap identification
│   └── Positions your work
├── Ch 3. Methods (10-15%)
│   ├── General to thesis (specific methods go in chapters)
│   └── Reproducibility artifacts
├── Ch 4-6+. Results / Contributions (40-60%)
│   ├── One distinct contribution per chapter
│   ├── Each chapter = self-contained: intro → methods → results → discussion
│   └── Chapter-level contributions link back to Ch1
├── Ch N. General Discussion (10-15%)
│   ├── Integrate chapters
│   ├── Limitations (honest)
│   └── Implications + future work
└── Ch N+1. Conclusion (2-5%)

Back matter
├── References (unified or per-chapter)
├── Appendices (supplementary methods, data, proofs)
└── (Sometimes: list of publications)
```

## Thesis-by-publication scaffold

```
Ch 1. Introduction
Ch 2. Background & Methods (overarching)
Ch 3. [Paper 1 — verbatim or reformatted]
Ch 4. [Paper 2]
Ch 5. [Paper 3]
Ch 6. Synthesis / General Discussion
    ├── How papers connect (critical!)
    ├── Contributions as a whole
    └── Future directions
Appendix. Author contributions (CRediT per paper)
```

Requirements vary: some universities need ≥3 first-author papers, others accept co-authored with contribution statement.

## Cambridge-specific

- Hard word limit: 60,000 words (STEM) or 80,000 (humanities), excluding appendices/bibliography. Exceeding requires Degree Committee approval.
- Declaration of originality mandatory.
- Viva (oral exam) with 1 internal + 1 external examiner, private, 2-4 hours.
- Outcomes: pass / minor corrections (3 months) / major (6-12 months) / revise & resubmit / fail.

## MIT-specific

- No hard uniform limit — department-set.
- Thesis committee (3-5) + thesis defense (public talk + private Q&A).
- Defense announcement posted 2+ weeks ahead.
- Final thesis deposited in DSpace@MIT.

## Harvard-specific

- Varies by GSAS department.
- Defense often public, followed by private exam.
- Dissertation submitted to ProQuest + Harvard DASH.

## Narrative arc principles

1. **One thesis** (noun): single answerable claim. State in ≤2 sentences in Ch1.
2. **Contributions numbered** (typically 3-5). Each must be new, demonstrated, and non-trivial.
3. **Chapters link**: forward references ("as shown in Ch5") + backward ("following Ch2").
4. **Hypothesis-driven** where applicable; exploratory labeled as such.

## Common failure modes

- **Kitchen sink**: every experiment ever done → no narrative
- **Stapled papers**: no synthesis between chapters
- **No contributions**: examiners can't state what's new
- **Overclaiming**: generalizing beyond data
- **Undercited recent work** (examiners check last 2 years)
- **Missing limitations** → examiners will find them in viva

## Defense / viva preparation

### Probable questions (prepare answers)

1. "Summarize your thesis in 3 minutes." — Practice verbatim.
2. "What are your contributions?" — Enumerate 3-5 crisply.
3. "Why this approach and not X?" — For each methodological choice.
4. "What's the weakest part of your thesis?" — Be honest; pre-empt.
5. "What would you do differently?" — Shows reflection.
6. "Limitations?" — Already in thesis; expand.
7. "How does this relate to [recent big paper]?" — Know last 12 months of field.
8. "What's next?" — Concrete 2-3 next projects.
9. Paper-specific deep dives — Be fluent in every figure + supplement.
10. "Defend assumption X." — For each key methodological assumption.

### Practice routines

- Mock viva with peers + supervisor
- 3-minute thesis (3MT) competition format
- Record answers, review for filler / hedging
- Anticipate examiner's own work — read their recent papers
- Prepare 1-page "thesis sheet" with contributions + figures to gesture to

### Defense day

- Bring printed thesis, tabbed to key figures
- Water, pen, notepad allowed
- If stuck: "That's a great question; let me think" is acceptable
- Honest "I don't know" >> fabricated answer
- Log post-defense corrections carefully

## Comprehensive / qualifying exam (pre-thesis)

Often first 1-2 years:
- Proposal defense (thesis plan review)
- Written exam on field breadth
- Oral exam with committee

Document what was examined — informs thesis scope.

## Publication strategy during thesis

- Target 1-2 first-author papers/year (field-dependent)
- Pre-prints (arXiv/bioRxiv/SSRN) recommended for priority
- Align papers with thesis chapters
- Clarify embargo with co-authors
