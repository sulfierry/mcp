# Priority, Embargo & Press

## Priority — who got there first

### Mechanisms that establish priority
1. **Preprint (bioRxiv/arXiv/medRxiv/ChemRxiv)** — date-stamped DOI, citable
2. **Conference abstract** — proceedings indexing (varies by field)
3. **Journal submission date** — tracked by publisher, not public until acceptance
4. **Publication date** — final, but comes last
5. **Patent filing** — separate legal priority

For academic priority in most fields, **preprint DOI wins**.

### Preprint timing
- Post as soon as manuscript is submitted to journal (or even a few days earlier)
- arXiv/bioRxiv assigns permanent DOI + version (v1)
- Subsequent versions preserve priority on v1 date
- Editors of nearly all top venues (Nature, Science, Cell, eLife) are now preprint-neutral or -positive
- Exception: NEJM still restricts preprinting for ongoing clinical trials

### "Scooped" scenarios
If someone else publishes before you:
- Your preprint establishes co-discovery if dates overlap
- Journals may accept as "independent replication" or "simultaneous publication"
- Email editor with your preprint DOI + timeline if concerned

### Avoiding being scooped
- Don't talk about unpublished data at seminars before preprint
- Preprint during peer review, not after acceptance
- Watch competitor preprint servers + Twitter/BlueSky / conference abstracts
- Register clinical trials / systematic reviews early (PROSPERO, CT.gov)

## Embargo management

### What is embargo
Journal decides: paper accepted but release date set. Media/you must not publicize until embargo lifts.

### Typical timelines
- Nature / Science / Cell: 1-2 week embargo before online publication
- NEJM / Lancet: up to 4 weeks
- Notification to press via EurekAlert! typically 1-3 days before embargo lifts

### What you can do during embargo
- Share with institution's press office (they can write release under embargo)
- Share with co-authors' press offices
- NOT: tweet, post preprint update, mention at conference, blog

### Breaking embargo
Serious consequences:
- Paper may be pulled/delayed
- Ban on future submissions to that journal
- Media lose access to journal's news service
- Rare but real examples: papers withdrawn over social-media leaks

### Embargo disclosure to funders / board
Usually allowed if they respect embargo. Explicit embargo statement in email.

## Press release craft

### When you'd write one
- Finding has broad public interest (health, environment, economy, security)
- Institution asks for one (most press offices)
- Field journal may offer co-release

### Who writes
Institution's press office (university / research institute / funder). YOU:
- Provide lay-summary draft (200-300 w non-specialist)
- Suggested headlines (3-5 options)
- Representative quotes from yourself + senior co-authors
- Fact-check their final version

### Release structure (for press office reference)
1. **Headline** — concrete, active, 10-15 words
2. **Lede** (first paragraph) — who/what/when/where/why (50 w)
3. **Quote from lead author** — human angle, 1-2 sentences
4. **Context** — why it matters (100 w)
5. **Methods/findings** — accessible (100 w)
6. **Quote from senior author** — bigger picture
7. **Limitations** — honest caveat
8. **Contact info**
9. **Embargo statement** — date/time + zone

### Lay-summary tips
- No jargon; define any remaining technical term immediately
- Concrete numbers (10 times stronger, 50% faster)
- Analogy to everyday experience
- One take-home sentence by paragraph 2
- Avoid: "groundbreaking", "revolutionary" — journalists distrust

## Media training (for interviews)

### Pre-interview
- Ask for interview topic, format, audience, deadline
- Get 3 bullet points you want to land (rehearse variations)
- Know: name/outlet of journalist, context of piece (Nature Podcast vs local TV)
- Prepare analogies for every technical term

### During
- Short answers (15-30 seconds for audio/video)
- Bridge back to bullet points if question drifts
- Admit uncertainty: "that's a good question; we don't know yet"
- Don't speculate beyond data
- Correct misrepresentations mid-interview

### Common traps
- "When will this be in clinics?" → honest timeline ("years of trials needed")
- "Is this a cure?" → rarely yes; distinguish prevention/treatment/cure
- Comparisons to other lab's work → neutral credit
- Political angles ("does this prove [ideology]?") → stay factual

### Nature Podcast specifics
- 5-10 min slot
- Producer pre-interview to find angle
- 2-3 key messages
- Co-hosts may press for "why does it matter"

## Social media strategy

### Accepted
- Twitter/BlueSky thread on publication day with key figures
- Tag journal, co-authors, institution
- Thread structure: 1/ headline → 2/ context → 3-6/ findings → 7/ limits → 8/ call-to-action (read, replicate)
- Pre-register or archive any public claims beyond paper

### Avoid
- Overclaiming ("this cures X")
- Engaging bad-faith critics in public threads
- Breaking embargo on informal posts
- Unfiltered reactions to criticism

## Scooping / simultaneous publication

If another lab posts competing preprint while yours is in review:
- Email editor + include your preprint + timeline
- Editor may: (a) proceed independently, (b) offer simultaneous publication, (c) request differentiation of scope
- Scientific community usually respects both papers

## Conference abstract priority

Field-dependent:
- CS (NeurIPS, ICML): conference paper = primary publication
- Life sciences: abstract secondary to journal paper
- Biomedical: ACM/IEEE differs from Cell/Nature

Check your field's norms before presenting unpublished data.

## Anti-patterns

- Tweet findings before preprint/acceptance
- Press release overclaim (retraction risk)
- Ignore institutional press office (they coordinate)
- Break embargo even by accident (always confirm dates)
- Speaking to journalists without media training → misquotes
- Assuming embargo protects all content (only the paper — slides/talks = separate)
- Claiming priority via "we presented at conference" when no abstract indexed
- Ignoring competitor preprints → surprise scoops
