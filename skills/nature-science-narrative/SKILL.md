# Nature/Science Narrative Craft

## When to use
- Targeting Nature / Science / Cell / PNAS (and family: Nature Medicine, Nature Biotech, Cell Metabolism, etc.)
- Writing abstract + first paragraph of Introduction
- Crafting the "take-home sentence"
- Editing an already-drafted paper for broad-interest fit

## Core principle: Inverted Pyramid

News-writing structure, not lab-report. Importance decreases top→bottom:

```
 1. Headline claim         ← MUST be front-loaded
 2. Broad implication      ← why any biologist/physicist/chemist cares
 3. Specific finding       ← what you discovered
 4. Evidence (brief)       ← how demonstrated
 5. Caveats                ← honest limits
 6. Future work            ← not why you care, what opens up
```

Lab-report order (methods → results → discussion) is WRONG for top-tier abstracts.

## The "one take-home sentence"

Every paper has ONE sentence. Write it before the abstract:

> "We show that [X], demonstrating that [broad implication] and enabling [future direction]."

If you can't complete this in 25 words, the paper isn't ready. If it needs "and also [Y]", split into two papers.

Examples (paraphrased from real Nature papers):
- "We solve the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, revealing the drug-target architecture for COVID-19 therapeutics."
- "We demonstrate that base editors can correct >90% of human pathogenic point mutations."
- "We observe gravitational waves from a binary neutron star merger, opening multi-messenger astronomy."

Note: each is structural (X solves Y enabling Z), not decorative.

## 150-word abstract anatomy (Nature unstructured)

| Sentences | Purpose | Word budget |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| 1-2 | Context: why field cares (broad) | 30 |
| 3 | The gap / unknown | 15 |
| 4-5 | What we did (1 sentence methods) | 25 |
| 6-7 | What we found (the results sentence) | 30 |
| 8-9 | What it means / enables (implication) | 30 |
| 10 | Final punch (future/opening) | 20 |

Avoid: abbreviations in sentence 1; "here we" until sentence 4; hedges ("may", "could suggest").

## Science structured abstract (250 words, 4 boxed sections)

```
INTRODUCTION: field context + problem (50-75 w)
RATIONALE: approach + why it works (50-75 w)
RESULTS: findings + magnitude (75-100 w)
CONCLUSION: implication + what it unlocks (50 w)
```

Each box must stand alone — readers scan.

## First paragraph of Introduction (top-tier)

Same inverted structure as abstract, expanded to 300 words:

1. **Hook** (1-2 sentences) — broad phenomenon all readers recognize.
2. **Specific question** (1-2) — narrows to your question.
3. **What's known** (2-3) — terse prior work.
4. **The gap** (1-2) — what's missing.
5. **Your contribution** (1-2) — "Here, we show..."
6. **Preview of implication** (1) — why this matters beyond the paper.

No lengthy literature review in para 1. Move that to para 2-3 or Methods.

## Broad-interest framing

Top-tier editors ask: "Would a physicist care about this biology paper?" / "Would a clinician care about this materials paper?"

Reframe findings for cross-field appeal:
- Biology finding about protein folding → "new principle for designing functional molecules"
- Physics discovery about phase transition → "template for understanding complex systems"
- Materials characterization → "method that generalizes to any X"

Avoid jargon in opening sentence. Technical vocabulary delayed until para 2.

## Voice and tone

- **Active** ("we show", "we observe") not passive ("it was shown")
- **Declarative** ("X causes Y") not tentative ("X may contribute to Y")
- **Present tense for claims** ("the receptor binds"), past for methods ("we crystallized")
- **Specific numbers** ("at 2.1 Å resolution") not vague ("at high resolution")
- **Contrastive** ("unlike previous [reference], we...") to make novelty explicit

## Title craft

| Pattern | Example |
|---------|---------|
| **Finding + entity** | "Structural basis of X" |
| **Claim as title** | "A single gene controls Y" |
| **Colon question→answer** | "How cells sense force: mechanotransduction via piezo channels" |
| **Counterintuitive / surprising** | "Silent mutations have loud effects" |

Max ~12-15 words. Avoid: "Insights into...", "Towards...", "Studies of..." — weak.

## Figure abstract / graphical abstract

Many top journals (Cell, Science) offer. Structure:
- **Visual**: single panel showing core finding
- **Caption**: one 25-word sentence with take-home

Treat as 5th reading layer (after title → abstract → figures → body).

## "Significance statement" (PNAS, some Nature journals)

50-120 words explicitly for non-experts:
- Why a general reader should care (no jargon)
- What's new vs prior
- Broader implication

Use grandmother test: if your grandmother can't follow the first sentence, rewrite.

## Anti-patterns (auto-rejects at top-tier)

- Abstract starts with "Recent studies have shown..."
- Laundry-list findings ("We show A, B, C, and D") — editor wants one big thing
- "Novel" (≥1 use/abstract auto-flagged; use "first" only if defensible)
- Acronyms before definition in sentence 1
- "In this paper, we..." (redundant at top-tier)
- Hedging stack ("may potentially suggest that...")
- Methods-first abstract structure
- Zero quantitative results in abstract
- "More work is needed" as closing — reserve for Discussion paragraph

## Checklist before submission

- [ ] One-take-home sentence fits in ≤25 words
- [ ] Abstract first sentence understandable to PhD in neighboring field
- [ ] Numbers in abstract (how many, how much, how fast)
- [ ] No jargon in sentence 1 or title
- [ ] Title is claim, not topic
- [ ] Zero "novel/new" adjectives
- [ ] Active voice in ≥80% of sentences
- [ ] Graphical abstract / significance statement drafted
- [ ] First paragraph ends with implication preview, not methods
